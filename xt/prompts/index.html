Letterform bank

The genre the model carves. Name it — the image won’t infer it.

BlackletterOld English, spiked terminals, dense verticals. The reference.
Geometric groteskEven strokes, closed apertures. Reads modern, corporate-clean.
High-contrast DidoneHairline serifs against fat stems. Luxury, editorial.
Extended slabWide, heavy, blunt. Signage weight, reads at distance.
Interlocked monogramTwo letters sharing a stem. Best for two-letter marks.
Script ligatureContinuous single stroke. Fluid, needs a glossy material.
StencilBroken counters, military/industrial. Great in brushed metal.
Chrome bubbleInflated soft-body letterform. Y2K, maximum specularity.

Ground & light

The reference uses the cheapest, most reliable pair. Change one at a time.

Pure white backgroundClean cut-out. Compositing-ready. The default.
Seamless black voidMaterial reads by highlight alone. Dramatic, riskier.
Soft studio gradientGentle falloff, product-shot feel.
High-key softbox lightingEven, shadowless, catalogue-clean.
Single hard key, long shadowSculptural. Shows the form’s depth.
Caustic light through the objectOnly worth it for glass and gemstone.

How to actually get better

  1. Change one clause per run. If you swap material and lighting together you learn nothing about either.
  2. Name the letterform. The reference image carries “blackletter” silently. Words don’t. Unnamed, you get whatever the model defaults to.
  3. Keep the ground as its own sentence. “Pure white background.” buried in a comma-list gets diluted.
  4. Brackets are a signal, not syntax. They mark the slot for you. Most models treat the contents as literal text to render — which is what you want for a monogram.
  5. Two letters beat five. Every generator degrades on long strings. Monogram the mark, set the wordmark in real type underneath.
  6. Save the ones that land, with their exact prompt. A result without its recipe is a dead end.
