01 — The spec

Seven requirements

1
One-shot from the lyrics
Hand it the song. Get a finished video back. Not a storyboard, not a shot list to assemble by hand — one pass, one output.
2
Photorealistic
Not illustrated, not stylized-by-default, not an anime filter hiding the seams. Real-looking footage.
3
His likeness locked in every frame
Not “resembles him in the first shot.” The same face, recognizably him, from the first frame to the last. This is the requirement everything else breaks against — see section 03.
4
Seamless continuation
Chunks that join without a visible reset — no jump in lighting, wardrobe, or face between one segment and the next.
5
Flat monthly price
Token metering is rejected outright. On the offer of 10 minutes a month for $29: “take it off the table.”
6
Generous usage
In his words, room to “generate to my heart’s content.” A cap that forces rationing per song defeats the purpose.
7
Fast
On two hours of render for a two-minute song: “that is dead.” Iteration speed is part of the product, not a nice-to-have.

02 — The chunking math

Worked out, reusable for any song length

The structure is right, and it is the same structure professionals use: cut the song into 30-second chunks, give each chunk 4 image anchors. For a two-minute song that is 4 chunks and 16 anchors. Thirty seconds is a sensible chunk because it is roughly a verse or a hook, and because — per section 03 — short segments are how drift is contained.

One number to pin down before generating anything

The chunk structure and the cut rhythm are two different dials, and the two figures describe different things. Sixteen anchors across 120 seconds is 7.5 seconds of screen time each — not 1.8–2.0. Both numbers can be right, but only under one of these readings:

Reading A — anchors, not cuts. The 16 images are anchor frames and a video model animates between them. Each anchor then owns 7.5s of motion, and the 1.8–2.0s figure is the perceived rhythm inside that — camera pushes, angle changes, transitions. 16 images is the correct count.

Reading B — hard cuts. Every image is its own shot, held ~2s, then cut. A two-minute song needs 60–67 images, not 16 — about 15–16 per 30s chunk.

Reading B is the one that runs tonight (section 05), and it is the far larger image order. Worth settling before the first batch, because the difference is 16 renders versus 64.

03 — Temporal drift, explained properly

Why the face stops being his face

This is the crux of the whole spec, and it is usually explained wrong. The wrong version says “use InstantID and your likeness is locked.” That sentence quietly swaps two different things.

InstantID and IP-Adapter are still-image tools

Both are SDXL tools. They take a face embedding and condition the generation of one image on it. They lock identity in a still. That is what they do, and they do it well.

The video model never learns his face

AnimateDiff, SVD, Wan — these animate a frame that already has the face in it. Nothing in their weights knows who he is. They know how pixels tend to move. So identity is locked at frame one of a clip, and from frame two onward the model's own priors take over and pull the face toward its generic average. That pull is temporal drift.

This is why professionals generate short shots. Not for aesthetics, and not because long shots look boring — because drift accumulates per frame, so the only reliable defense is to stop and re-anchor before it becomes visible. Short shots are drift management.

The two mitigations, and why neither works alone

Mitigation 1 — last-frame chaining

Take the final frame of clip N and use it as the first frame of clip N+1. Wan 2.1 supports this natively. UNVERIFIED — documented capability, not run on this machine.

What it buys: continuity across the seam. Wardrobe, lighting, and pose carry over, so the chunks join without a reset. That is requirement 4.

What it does not buy: identity. Chaining re-anchors on an already slightly drifted frame, so error compounds across chunks — the middle row above. Over 4 chunks it is survivable. Over 8 it is not.

Mitigation 2 — a trained face LoRA

Fine-tune a small adapter on a set of his photos so the model itself knows his face. UNVERIFIED — not attempted on this machine.

What it buys: identity that regenerates. Every frame is pulled back toward him instead of away, because the likeness lives in the weights rather than in one conditioning image.

What it does not buy: continuity. A LoRA will happily give him the right face in the wrong jacket, in different light, at a different time of day, in the very next clip.

They are not alternatives

Chaining without a LoRA holds the scene together while the face slips. A LoRA without chaining holds the face while the scene jumps. Requirements 3 and 4 need both at once — that is the single most important sentence on this page, and it is the part most write-ups leave out.

The Hetzner correction

64 GB of RAM will not run AnimateDiff

System RAM is not VRAM. They are different memory, attached to different processors, and a diffusion model needs the second kind. Sixty-four gigabytes of system RAM on a CPU-only box runs AnimateDiff the way a large fuel tank runs a car with no engine.

Standard Hetzner cloud instances have no GPU at all. Their GPU line — GEX, RTX 4000 Ada — is a separate and pricier product. The 64 GB box is not on it. VERIFIED

Neither Hetzner host will take his SSH key. Both returned Permission denied (publickey) today. Nothing can be tested there until that is fixed. VERIFIED

Storage is 124 GB on a disk he already called prone to fail. SDXL plus AnimateDiff plus SVD weights alone run roughly 20–50 GB before a single output frame is written. UNVERIFIED on the weight sizes.

The one command that settles it on any box, before believing any spec sheet: run nvidia-smi. Output means a GPU. No output means no GPU, and no amount of system RAM changes that answer.

05 — The honest gap, and the path that runs tonight

Said plainly

The full spec does not exist on a 24 GB M4 today

One-shot from lyrics, photorealistic, likeness locked in every frame, seamless, fast, free, and local — all seven at once is not available on this machine. Not with a better prompt, not with a different checkpoint, not tonight.

Paid does not close it either. No service today delivers one-shot lyrics-to-photoreal video with a genuinely locked likeness. The one-shot requirement is the hardest one in the list, and it is unmet across the board.

That is the part worth being blunt about, because there is a paying client on the flyer commission and a wrong recommendation costs them, not just him.

What does run tonight

The still-based path. It is not a downgrade of the spec — it satisfies the hardest requirement on the list outright, and it does so by construction rather than by mitigation.

1
Generate the stills. SDXL-class images with InstantID or IP-Adapter carrying his face. Every image gets looked at and approved before it goes in.
2
Chunk them per his math. 30-second sections, batch per section. Reading B from section 02 — roughly 15–16 images per chunk, 60–67 for a two-minute song.
3
Animate each still. Slow push-in, pan, parallax, rack focus. ffmpeg does this on CPU in seconds per shot — no model, no waiting.
4
Cut on the beat. Get the beat grid from the track, hand ffmpeg the timing list, and every transition lands on a hit instead of near one.
5
Mux and ship. Audio in, one file out. Iterate a shot by replacing one image and re-running — not by re-rendering the video.

Why this is the strong option, not the consolation

Zero temporal drift. Structurally, not approximately. Every frame that reaches the screen is a still he already approved. There is no video model in the loop pulling the face toward its average, so there is nothing to drift. Requirement 3 — the one that breaks every other approach — is the one this path satisfies most completely.

It is fast where it counts. The slow step is image generation, which is parallel and restartable. The assembly step is ffmpeg, which is seconds. Fixing shot 7 means regenerating one image, not two hours of video.

It is flat and unlimited, at zero. Requirements 5 and 6, met outright.

The honest trade, stated so no one is surprised

This reads as animated photography — moving stills, cut to music — not continuous photorealistic motion. It is a recognized and respected music-video language, but it is a different look, and it is not requirement 1 or requirement 4.

For the flyer commission this is likely the right call regardless: a short piece built from approved frames is more controllable, more on-brand, and safer to put in front of a client than a video model that may hand back a face that is nearly him.

06 — What would change the answer

Three conditions, and how to test each

A real GPU
An NVIDIA card with 24 GB or more of VRAM — 4090 or A6000 class — owned or rented by the hour. This is the condition that unlocks AnimateDiff, Wan, SVD, and face-LoRA training in one move. If Hetzner stays in the picture, it has to be the GEX line, not the 64 GB box. The SSH key rejection has to be cleared first either way.
Test: first command on any box, before believing a single line of the spec sheet — nvidia-smi. No output, no GPU, walk away.
A flat-rate renderer with commercial rights
Four things have to be true together, and vendors tend to offer three: a genuinely flat monthly price with no per-second or per-token metering; a usage ceiling high enough to iterate a whole song; an explicit commercial-use grant, because there is a paying client; and a real identity-lock feature — a trained character, not a reference-image upload, which is frame-one conditioning under a friendlier name. UNVERIFIED — no vendor was evaluated tonight.
Test: read the rights clause before any client work goes through it, and price the plan against a full song’s worth of re-rolls, not one clean render.
An MLX-native video model
MLX is Apple’s native Apple-Silicon framework. Today’s video diffusion stack is CUDA-shaped, which is the real reason the local path is unproven — not the 24 GB. A solid MLX port of a Wan or LTX-class model would change the local answer with no hardware purchase at all. This is the one to watch, because it arrives as a download rather than an invoice.
Test: the day an MLX video model ships, re-run section 04 — that table is the thing that would change, and nothing else on this page has to.
A shorter deliverable — already true
Not a future condition, just a reframe worth having on the table. A 15–30 second flyer is a different problem from a 2–4 minute music video: one or two chunks, a handful of anchors, and drift never gets time to accumulate. The commission is reachable on the tonight path without waiting on any of the three above. The full-length music video is the piece that waits.
