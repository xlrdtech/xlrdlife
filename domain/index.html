Which version of the Transfer Policy governs — read this first

Getting the version right is the difference between a letter that works and one a compliance desk can wave off.

The governing document is the Transfer Policy as updated 21 February 2024. ICANN’s own note on it reads, verbatim:
“Updated 21 February 2024 to reflect changes required to implement the Registration Data Policy. Contracted parties may implement this updated Policy beginning on 21 August 2024 and must implement no later than 21 August 2025.”
The prior version is listed as “Effective 1 December 2016 through 20 August 2025.” The mandatory-implementation date of 21 August 2025 is a year past, so the 21 February 2024 text binds Wix in full today.

1. The unlock obligation, and the clock on it

Section I.A.5 of the Transfer Policy is titled Requirements for the “ClientTransferProhibited” Status and “AuthInfo” Codes. Verbatim:

§5.2
“Registrars must provide the Registered Name Holder with the unique ‘AuthInfo’ code and remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ within five (5) calendar days of the Registered Name Holder’s initial request if the Registrar does not provide facilities for the Registered Name Holder to generate and manage their own unique ‘AuthInfo’ code and to remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status.”
§5.3
“Registrars may not employ any mechanism for complying with a Registered Name Holder’s request to remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status or obtain the applicable ‘AuthInfo Code’ that is more restrictive than the mechanisms used for changing any aspect of the Registered Name Holder’s contact or name server information.”

So: five calendar days from the request, and the process they put in front of you may be no harder than changing a nameserver. A support queue that never resolves is itself the violation.

2. The clause that decides this case

§5.4
“The Registrar of Record must not refuse to remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status or release an ‘AuthInfo Code’ to the Registered Name Holder solely because there is a dispute between the Registered Name Holder and the Registrar over payment.”

This is the strongest sentence available and it lands directly on tonight’s facts. A failed renewal charge is a payment dispute. Under §5.4 a payment dispute is not a permitted reason to keep the lock on or withhold the code. The failed payment does not weaken the demand — it triggers the clause written for exactly this situation.

Reinforced by §3.10:

“The Registrar of Record has other mechanisms available to collect payment from the Registered Name Holder that are independent from the Transfer process. Hence, in the event of a dispute over payment, the Registrar of Record must not employ transfer processes as a mechanism to secure payment for services from a Registered Name Holder.”

3. The only grounds on which a transfer may be denied

§3.7 is a closed list — “The Registrar of Record may deny a transfer request only in the following specific instances” — and it carries a duty to explain: “Upon denying a transfer request for any of the following reasons, the Registrar of Record must provide the Registered Name Holder and the potential Gaining Registrar with the reason for denial.” Verbatim, all six:

3.7.1 “Evidence of fraud.”

3.7.2 “Reasonable dispute over the identity of the Registered Name Holder.”

3.7.3 “No payment for previous registration period (including credit card charge-backs) if the domain name is past its expiration date or for previous or current registration periods if the domain name has not yet expired. In all such cases, however, the domain name must be put into ‘Registrar Hold’ status by the Registrar of Record prior to the denial of transfer.”

3.7.4 “Express objection to the transfer by the authorized Registered Name Holder… upon request by the authorized Registered Name Holder, the Registrar must remove the lock or provide a reasonably accessible method for the authorized Registered Name Holder to remove the lock within five (5) calendar days.”

3.7.5 “The transfer was requested within 60 days of the creation date as shown in the registry RDDS record for the domain name.”

3.7.6 “A domain name is within 60 days (or a lesser period to be determined) after being transferred…”

And §3.9 lists what may never be a reason. Two of them are directly on point:

3.9.1 “Nonpayment for a pending or future registration period.”

3.9.3 “Domain name in Registrar Lock Status, unless the Registered Name Holder is provided with the reasonable opportunity and ability to unlock the domain name prior to the Transfer Request.”

The precise answer on nonpayment — past period vs. future period

This is the crux, so here it is exactly:

  • The registration is paid through 2026-08-31. That is what the registry expiry date means.
  • The charge that failed on 2026-08-01 is the renewal for the period beginning 2026-08-31 — a pending or future registration period.
  • §3.9.1 puts that squarely on the may-never-deny list. Nonpayment for a pending or future period is not a permitted basis for denial. Full stop.
  • §3.7.3 only ever reaches previous or current periods. Both are paid here, so it does not apply at all.
  • Even if Wix tried to invoke §3.7.3 anyway, it requires Registrar Hold status to be set before denial. Public whois shows ACTIVE. The precondition is absent on the public record, which is why the whois output is worth attaching to anything you send.

Short version: an unpaid future period cannot block the transfer; only an unpaid past or current period could, and there isn’t one.

4. Is the lock even allowed to be there?

§5.1, verbatim:

“Registrars may only set a domain name in ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status upon registration or subsequent request by the Registered Name Holder, provided, however, that the Registrar includes in its registration agreement (obtaining the express consent of the Registered Name Holder) the terms and conditions upon which it prohibits transfer of the domain name. Further, the Registrar must remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status within five (5) calendar days of the Registered Name Holder’s initial request if the Registrar does not provide facilities for the Registered Name Holder to remove the ‘ClientTransferProhibited’ status.”

So the answer is split, and the split is the whole point:

  • Applying it by default is permitted — but only at registration, and only if the registration agreement obtained express consent to the terms on which transfer is prohibited.
  • It must be removable on request, within five calendar days, and by a route no more onerous than editing a nameserver (§5.3). A default lock that cannot practically be lifted is not a lock the policy allows.

clientUpdateProhibited is not addressed by the Transfer Policy at all — it is an EPP status code, and per ICANN’s status-code reference it “tells your domain’s registry to reject requests to update the domain,” meaning nameservers and contact data are frozen on top of the transfer block. It does not independently prevent a transfer, but it does mean the domain cannot even be pointed elsewhere in the meantime, and it should be named in the demand so both come off together.

6. What actually happens at expiry CORRECTED

The earlier version of this page said “Aug 31 — hard wall, domain expires. Nothing lands here.” That was wrong in both directions, and getting it right changes the plan.

August 31 is not the moment the domain dies. Under the .com Registry Agreement, a name not renewed by its expiry date “will be automatically renewed by the system the first day after the expiration date,” opening an Auto-Renew Grace Period whose “current value… is 45 calendar days.” A transfer during that window still works, and still adds the year:

“If a domain is transferred within the Auto-Renew Grace Period, the losing Registrar is credited with the Auto-Renew charge and the year added by the Auto-Renew operation is cancelled. The expiration date of the domain is extended by one year up to a total maximum of ten and the gaining Registrar is charged for that additional year…”

ICANN says the same thing to registrants directly, in its FAQ for domain-name holders — question: “My domain name has just expired. Can my registrar require me to pay for a renewal before I can transfer the domain name to a new registrar?”

“No. You have the right to transfer an expired domain. Registrars are not allowed to deny a transfer due to expiration or nonrenewal, (unless you haven’t paid for a previous registration period).”

ICANN, FAQ for Domain-Name Holders, page dated 10 October 2017 — icann.org/resources/pages/name-holder-faqs-2017-10-10-en

But the real wall is worse than a date, because it is Wix’s choice and it can come early. If Wix deletes the registration, it goes straight to REDEMPTIONPERIOD — 30 calendar days — and there the Expired Registration Recovery Policy is absolute:

“During the Redemption Grace Period, the registry must disable DNS resolution and prohibit attempted transfers of the registration.”

ICANN, Expired Registration Recovery Policy §3.2, 28 February 2013 — icann.org/en/contracted-parties/consensus-policies/expired-registration-recovery-policy/expired-registration-recovery-policy-28-02-2013-en

Once it is in redemption, no letter and no complaint can move it — recovery runs through Wix, at Wix’s price. There is also a smaller trap worth knowing: under §2.2.3.2 the gaining registrar’s authorization form expires if “the domain name expires before the inter-registrar transfer is completed,” so a transfer straddling August 31 has to be re-authorized.

ICANN says the discretion part out loud, on the intake page for its own Renewal/Redemption complaint form — which makes this the cleanest available citation for why August 31 has to be treated as the deadline:

“After a domain name expires, the registrar may offer a period of 0-45 days for the registrant to renew it before it is deleted from the registrar database or re-registered by someone else. If the domain name is deleted, it will enter into a 30-day redemption period (exception: sponsored TLDs). The registrar’s practice in this regard will depend on its own domain name deletion and auto-renew policies.”

ICANN Contractual Compliance, Renewal / Redemption complaint form intake page — icann-nsp.my.site.com/compliance/s/renewal-redemption, retrieved 2026-08-01. Same page notes ICANN handles these under both the Expired Registration Recovery Policy and the Expired Domain Deletion Policy, and that “ICANN is not a registrar and cannot renew or redeem a domain name.”

“May offer” and “0-45” are doing all the work in that sentence. The floor is zero days, and which number you get is Wix’s policy choice, not your right.

So the deadline is behavioral, not legal. The law stays on your side past expiry. What does not survive is your control over the outcome, because after August 31 the decision to delete belongs to the registrar you are in a dispute with. Treat August 31 as the deadline even though the policy would let you argue later.

7. A transfer adds a year — verified verbatim

“Upon change of sponsorship of the registration of a Registered Name from one Registrar to another, according to Part A of the ICANN Policy on Transfer of Registrations between Registrars, the term of registration of the Registered Name shall be extended by one year, provided that the maximum term of the registration as of the effective date of sponsorship change shall not exceed ten years.”

.com Registry Agreement between ICANN and VeriSign, effective 1 December 2024 — full text (index: icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/com). Retrieved 2026-08-01. The same document obliges the gaining registrar to pay for it: “For each transfer of the sponsorship of a domain-name registration under the Transfer Policy, Registrar agrees to pay Verisign the renewal registration fee associated with a one-year extension.”

Confirmed, with one honest qualification. For .com this is a binding registry-level term, not a courtesy — so yes, the transfer rescues the domain and escapes Wix in a single action. The qualification: it is a registry rule, not an ICANN consensus policy, so it holds for .com specifically rather than universally. At the protocol layer the added term is an optional element the gaining registrar sends — RFC 5731 §3.2.4 describes “an OPTIONAL <domain:period> element that contains the number of units to be added to the registration period of the domain object at completion of the transfer process” — but the .com agreement makes the one-year extension mandatory on change of sponsorship regardless.

And be clear-eyed about the money: the year is not free, it is billed by the gaining registrar. You pay Spaceship roughly a year’s registration and receive the year plus the exit. Paying Wix the same order of money buys the year and keeps the lock.

What Wix owes, and what it does not

Worth being scrupulous here, because overstating this would undercut everything above.

Wix has no obligation to renew — say so plainly

“Subject to applicable consensus policies and provisions of the Registrar Accreditation Agreement (‘RAA’), registrars may delete registrations at any time after they expire.”

ICANN Expired Registration Recovery Policy §2.2.1

A failed payment followed by deletion is not, by itself, a violation of anything. Any argument built on “they were required to keep it alive” is simply wrong, and making it would hand Wix an easy win on a side issue. Do not make it.

But Wix does have enforceable notification duties

These are real, they are separate from the transfer obligation, and a breach is its own filing:

  • Two notices before expiry — ERRP §2.1.1 requires notifying the holder “at least two times,” one “approximately one month prior to expiration” and one “approximately one week prior.” ICANN’s implementation guidance fixes the compliant windows at 26–35 days and 4–10 days before expiry.
  • A third notice after expiry — §2.1.2: if the registration is neither renewed nor deleted “within five days after the expiration,” the registrar “must transmit at least one additional expiration notice… that includes instructions for renewing the registration.”
  • Delivery that does not require chasing — §2.1.3: notices must be in the language of the registration agreement and sent “in a manner, such as by email, that does not require affirmative action to receive.”
  • He must be permitted to renew — §2.2.5: from expiry through the DNS-interruption period, “the RAE must be permitted by the registrar to renew the expired registration.”
  • He must be permitted to redeem — §3.3: registrars “must permit the RAE to redeem a deleted registration during RGP.”
  • Fees must be disclosed — §4.1, quoted above.
Concretely, for a 2026-08-31 expiry: the one-month notice was due roughly 2026-07-27 → 2026-08-05, and the one-week notice roughly 2026-08-21 → 2026-08-27. Save every Wix email with full headers, and note the absence of any that never arrive.

The obligation that actually carries this

The exposure is the lock and the code, not the lapse. §I.A.5.2 is a hard five-day duty; §I.A.5.4 removes payment as an excuse; §I.A.3.9.1 removes the future-period debt as an excuse; and §I.A.3.7.3’s Registrar Hold precondition — unmet on the face of the public whois — removes the last one. Narrow, fully cited, and hard to argue with. Keep the case there.

Step 3 — transfer at Spaceship

With both statuses off and the EPP code in hand, start the inbound transfer. The registration gains a year on completion (§7 above). Approve the confirmation email immediately — the authorization form has a 60-day life and dies early if the domain expires first (§2.2.3.2). Expect up to five calendar days at the registry, since under §6.2 “the Registry Operator shall complete the requested transfer unless, within five (5) calendar days, Registry Operator receives a NACK protocol command from the Registrar of Record.”

Do not change registrant contact fields at any point in this process. Editing the registrant name, organization or email can trigger the mandatory 60-day Change-of-Registrant lock under §3.8.5 and hand Wix a legitimate reason to refuse.

Why this was invisible for two years

Nothing in the Wix interface says “we have locked your domain against transfer.” The transfer simply fails, and a failure with no stated reason reads as your own mistake — which is precisely what §3.7’s duty to give a reason exists to prevent. This was never a competence problem. It was a registry-level lock, a five-day obligation nobody invoked, and a duty to explain that was never honored. The record exonerates.
