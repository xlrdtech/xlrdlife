Frustrated Tech Demands Clashed With Business Dreams and Mother's Care

My wearable wrote that sentence about my day. It also named, in twelve words, the exact problem the caregiving software industry has spent a decade failing to solve.

EAST · CTO East

Most founders never get to see their own day measured. I do, because I instrumented mine — and the data says something the industry should be paying attention to.

Here is what that day contained.

A typeface failed to load. Two display classes declared no font and silently inherited the wrong one, so a headline shipped wearing a stranger's clothes. Hours went into finding it. Every technical founder recognises this cost and almost no one measures it.

In the same hours: three companies that exist as filings not yet made, and receivables not yet collected — because collecting means composing the message, and composition is the expensive operation when your brain fights text.

And in the same hours, my mother needed things. Not dramatic things. The ordinary, relentless, unbillable things.

Three claims on one day. None optional. None willing to wait their turn. Which raises the question the entire productivity industry has quietly declined to answer:

What does software owe the person whose day has three owners and no spare hands?

I would have written “juggled.” Juggling implies competence. It implies the objects agree to be juggled.

The word the summary chose was clashed, and I keep turning that over, because it's more accurate than anything I would have written myself.

Clashed is honest. These things don't take turns. The font breaks at the same hour the payment is late at the same hour someone needs to be driven somewhere. Time doesn't stretch to accommodate how much of it you need. You just pick, and the two you didn't pick sit there getting louder.

I'm dyslexic. Reading is expensive for me — text scrambles when I try to hold it still. I have ADHD and narcolepsy on top of that. So I build everything voice-first, because a system I have to read is a system I can't use.

For a long time I thought that was a workaround. A concession. The compromised version of the real thing.

It isn't. It's the whole design premise. If a tool only works for someone with quiet hands, clear eyes and an uninterrupted hour, it isn't a tool — it's a test, and it's testing the wrong thing. I'm not building around a limitation. I'm building for the actual conditions people are in when they need help most: hands full, attention split, someone calling from the other room.

The caregiving software I'm working on isn't a market opportunity I identified. It's a Tuesday I keep having.

The thing nobody tells you about caregiving is that it doesn't announce itself. There's no moment where you sign up. You don't become a caregiver — you just look up one day and notice you've been one for a while, and that it's been quietly rearranging every other thing in your life to fit around it.

It doesn't go on the calendar. It doesn't get invoiced. It doesn't appear in the story you tell about why the business is behind. So the day reads like failure — he didn't ship, he didn't collect, he didn't close — when a third of it went somewhere real and load-bearing and completely invisible to every system built to measure a day.

That's the part that got me about the sentence. A machine listened to my day and put the three things side by side, at equal weight, without ranking them. No productivity system I've ever used would do that. They'd all show me the same thing: tasks not completed.

I didn't set out to build a product from this. The product is just what the collision left behind.

Every piece of software I was handed assumed I had a free hand and a quiet minute. Open the app. Log the thing. Tap to confirm. Read this screen. Every one of those instructions is a small tax, and the tax comes due at exactly the moment you cannot pay it — when someone needs you now, when your hands are full, when the words on the screen won't sit still.

So at CTO East we built for the opposite conditions. Not the demo conditions. The real ones.

Voice first, because I can't read my way through a crisis and neither can most people in one.

Hands free, because the moments that most need capturing are the moments both hands are already busy.

No confirmation taps, because every tap is a place where a tired person gives up. If the system needs a human to press something before it works, it doesn't work.

And it listens rather than asks. That's the piece I keep coming back to tonight. The reason that summary landed on me is that nothing had to be entered for it to exist. I didn't log my day. I lived it, and the record assembled itself, and at the end it told me the truth about it.

A caregiver should not also have to be a data entry clerk for their own life.

This is not a finished story, and I would not trust one that claimed to be. The filings are in progress. The receivables are in progress. The work continues while the thesis gets tested against real days.

But the thesis itself is settled, and it did not come from market research. It came from running the experiment on the only subject who would let me instrument every hour of it.

If you are in the same collision — building, owing, caring, all inside one twenty-four hours — the useful reframe is this: it is a design failure, not a personal one. The tools were built for conditions you do not have. That is a solvable problem, and it is the one we are solving.

Which means the metric most software reports on you is measuring the wrong thing.

It's that all three things still had someone showing up for them.
